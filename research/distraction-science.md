# Distraction science behind grod

What the research says about short-form feeds and about tools that try to curb them, and how that shapes grod's design. Each paper was opened at its source (paper or abstract page) on 2026-11-06. Most of the harm evidence is correlational or from small samples: treat it as motivation, not proof.

## Harms of short-form video and infinite scroll

- **Chiossi, Haliburton, Ou, Butz, Schmidt (2023).** "Short-Form Videos Degrade Our Capacity to Retain Intentions: Effect of Context Switching On Prospective Memory." CHI '23. https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580778. In a study with 60 people split into groups, only the TikTok group got worse at remembering a planned action; Twitter and YouTube did not. The authors attribute this to short clips combined with rapid context switching.
- **Nguyen et al. (2025).** "Feeds, Feelings, and Focus: Cognitive and Mental Health Correlates of Short-Form Video Use." *Psychological Bulletin* 151(9). https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000498. A meta-analysis of 71 studies (N≈98k). Heavier use goes with poorer attention and inhibitory control (r≈−.34) and with more anxiety and stress. Addiction-like use is a stronger warning sign than raw time spent.
- **Yan et al. (2024).** *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2024.1383913. EEG with the attention network test: people more prone to short-video addiction showed weaker self-control and weaker executive attention.
- **Chen et al. (2023).** *Behaviour & Information Technology* 42(16). Eye-tracking plus a Stroop task with addicted and non-addicted Douyin users: the addicted group had more trouble keeping attention on task.
- **Sharma, Lee, Johnson (2022).** "The Dark at the End of the Tunnel: Doomscrolling on Social Media Newsfeeds." *Technology, Mind, and Behavior* 3(1). https://doi.org/10.1037/tmb0000059. A validated scale for doomscrolling, which is linked to anxiety, passive use and poor self-control.
- **Ruiz, Molina León, Heuer (2024).** "Design Frictions on Social Media." https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18803. People who had to react to each post before seeing more remembered more than people on infinite scroll, but most found the friction annoying.

## Digital self-control interventions

- **Lyngs et al. (2019).** "Self-Control in Cyberspace." CHI '19. https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300361. A review of 367 tools: most block or track; few remove the trigger itself.
- **Lyngs et al. (2020).** "I Just Want to Hack Myself to Not Get Distracted." CHI '20. https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04180. Over 6 weeks (N=58), removing the Facebook newsfeed reduced distraction but caused fear of missing out. The authors recommend letting people block what *they* define as distracting.
- **Purohit et al. (2023).** "Starving the Newsfeed for Social Media Detox." CHI '23. https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581187. Field experiment (N=138): removing the feed cut Facebook time by 64%; a reduced feed cut it by 39%.
- **Zhang, Lukoff, Rao, Baughan, Hiniker (2022).** "Monitoring Screen Time or Redesigning It?" CHI '22. https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3517722. In a Twitter client study, redesigning the interface raised users' sense of agency; usage dashboards did not.
- **Lukoff et al. (2021).** "How the Design of YouTube Influences User Sense of Agency." CHI '21. https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.11778. Recommendations and autoplay lower users' sense of control; search and playlists raise it.
- **Grüning, Riedel, Lorenz-Spreen (2023).** "Directing smartphone use through the self-nudge app one sec." *PNAS* 120(8). https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2213114120. Over 6 weeks (N=280), users backed out about 36% of the time and opening attempts fell 57%. The back-out option and the delay did the work; the reflective message alone did not. The lead author is affiliated with one sec.
- **Kovacs, Wu, Bernstein (2018).** "Rotating Online Behavior Change Interventions Increases Effectiveness But Also Increases Attrition." CSCW '18. https://doi.org/10.1145/3274364. Rotating interventions cut time but nearly doubled uninstalls; a short explanation at the moment of change roughly halved that.
- **Kovacs, Wu, Bernstein (2021).** "Not Now, Ask Later." CHI '21. https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.11743. Users gradually switch to easier interventions while planning to make them stricter "later."
- **Kim, Jung, Ko, Lee (2019).** "GoalKeeper." IMWUT 3(1). Hard lockouts work better than warnings but frustrate people more.
- **Okeke, Sobolev, Dell, Estrin (2018).** "Good Vibrations." MobileHCI '18. https://doi.org/10.1145/3229434.3229463. A vibration nudge cut Facebook time by more than 20%, but use returned to baseline once the nudge was removed.
- **Monge Roffarello & De Russis (2023).** "Achieving Digital Wellbeing Through Digital Self-control Tools." ACM TOCHI 30(4). https://doi.org/10.1145/3571810. These tools work in the short term, but the effect fades.
- **Monge Roffarello & De Russis (2023).** "Nudging Users or Redesigning Interfaces?" GoodIT '23. https://doi.org/10.1145/3582515.3609523. Both a nudge and a redesign reduced passive scrolling.
- **Fitz, Kushlev, Jagannathan, Lewis, Paliwal, Ariely (2019).** "Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being." *Computers in Human Behavior* 101. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016. Field experiment, N=237. Getting notifications three times a day improved attention, productivity and mood. No notifications at all raised anxiety and fear of missing out. Hourly batching helped little.
- **Meinhardt et al. (2025).** "Scrolling in the Deep." https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11814. Context matters: being at home in a bad mood made interventions work more slowly.
- **Meinhardt, Dragic, Colley, Lukoff, Rukzio (2026).** "Can't Stop: Gradual Interventions for Infinite Scrolling on Short-Form Video Platforms." IMWUT. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15818. 7 days, N=104. People learn to ignore simple pop-ups; gradually increasing visual friction stays effective longest.
- **Holte & Ferraro (2020).** "True colors: Grayscale setting reduces screen time in college students." *The Social Science Journal* 60(2). https://doi.org/10.1080/03623319.2020.1737461. N=161 students, 73 of them on grayscale for 8–10 days. Their total screen time fell by about 38 minutes a day, from less social media and browsing; video time did not change. Anxiety and depression improved in both groups, so that part was not due to grayscale. Students only, short period.
- **Castelo, Kushlev, Ward, Esterman, Reiner (2025).** "Blocking mobile internet on smartphones improves sustained attention, mental health, and subjective well-being." *PNAS Nexus* 4(2). https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf017. Randomized trial with a waitlist control, N=467 iPhone users. Two weeks without mobile internet (calls and texts kept) improved sustained attention, mental health and well-being; 91% improved on at least one. Only 25.5% fully kept the block on, participants were already motivated to cut back, and the authors note that expectancy effects may have contributed. Removing everything works for the few who keep it up.

## Asking why, and adapting the friction

Sources opened on 2026-11-07. Abstract-only sources say so.

- **Kim, Park, Lee, Ko, Lee (2019).** "LocknType." CHI '19. https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300927. 3-week field experiment, N=40. A pause with only a continue button stopped 13.1% of app openings; typing a 30-digit number stopped 47.5%. Short, and lasting effects were not measured.
- **Xu et al. (2022).** "TypeOut." CHI '22. https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3517476. 10 weeks, within-subject, N=54. Typing a self-affirmation to unlock cut app use by more than half and openings and session length by more than a quarter, beating random typing and a shown-but-not-typed affirmation. Read at the abstract only.
- **Terzimehić, Haliburton, Greiner, Schmidt, Hußmann, Mäkelä (2022).** "MindPhone." DIS '22. https://doi.org/10.1145/3532106.3533575. 2 weeks, N=28. Asking "what will you do on the phone?" at unlock did not change screen time; asking "what will you do in the real world afterwards?" lowered screen time and unlocks. Retyping the same purpose for message checks annoyed people. Small sample, hand-copied screen time.
- **Terzimehić, Bemmann, Halsner, Mayer (2023).** "A Mixed-Method Exploration into the Mobile Phone Rabbit Hole." PACM HCI 7 (MobileHCI). https://doi.org/10.1145/3604241. N=21, 14,395 sessions. Messaging was the most stated purpose; 25.8% of labeled sessions went beyond their purpose and lasted 16.4 min against 4.2 min. Only 1,738 sessions were labeled.
- **Lukoff, Yu, Kientz, Hiniker (2018).** "What Makes Smartphone Use Meaningful or Meaningless?" IMWUT 2(1). https://doi.org/10.1145/3191754. N=45, 86,402 sessions. Purpose-driven use fell from 65.6% at the start of a session to 51.4% during it; habitual use felt less meaningful. Correlational.
- **Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006).** *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1. Meta-analysis of 94 tests: if-then plans raised goal attainment (d=.65), across behaviors other than phone use. Against it for phones: **Brockmeier et al. (2025)**, *Computers in Human Behavior* 168, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108624, preregistered RCT, N=787: action and coping plans did not change measured phone time. Both read at the abstract only.
- **Mertens et al. (2025).** "Wellspent." *JMIR mHealth and uHealth* 14. https://doi.org/10.2196/56824. RCT, 3 weeks, N=70. A full-screen quit-or-continue screen after a self-set session limit cut time on the worst app by about 29 min a day, without changing problematic use scores. Self-reported screen time.
- **Orzikulova et al. (2024).** "Time2Stop." CHI '24. https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05584. 8 weeks, N=71. Machine learning chose when to intervene and learned from feedback; app visits fell 7–9%. It adapted timing, not the length of the friction. Framework: **Nahum-Shani et al. (2018)**, *Annals of Behavioral Medicine* 52(6), https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-016-9830-8 (just-in-time adaptive interventions), read at the abstract only.
- **Vance, Kirwan, Bjornn, Jenkins, Anderson (2017).** "What Do We Really Know about How Habituation to Warnings Occurs Over Time?" CHI '17. https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025896. fMRI and eye tracking over 5 days, N=16: attention to a repeated warning fell across the week; warnings that changed appearance resisted it. Security warnings in a lab. Review support: **Biedermann et al. (2021)**, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* 37(5), https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12581: blocks that are easy to get past are quickly dismissed (abstract only).
- grod's own data before the opening check: 37 openings through the launcher's fixed 10 s pause, 0 back-outs.

No study found tested a wait whose length depends on the previous session or the day's use; grod's Browse wait growing with the day's Browse answers is an extrapolation from LocknType, Vance and Meinhardt 2026.

## Notifications and phone presence

- **Stothart, Mitchum, Yehnert (2015).** "The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification." *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance* 41(4). https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100. A notification that was not answered hurt performance on an attention task about as much as taking a call or texting. Lab task only. A preregistered experiment (Johannes, Veling, Verwijmeren, Buijzen 2019, *Journal of Media Psychology* 31(4), https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-1105/a000248) found that notifications made students feel more distracted but did not change their response-inhibition scores.
- **Kushlev, Proulx, Dunn (2016).** "'Silence Your Phones': Smartphone Notifications Increase Inattention and Hyperactivity Symptoms." CHI '16. https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858359. N=221, one week with alerts on and the phone in reach, one week with alerts off and the phone away. People reported more inattention and hyperactivity in the alerts-on week. Self-report, and alerts and phone distance changed together, so their effects cannot be separated.
- **Pielot & Rello (2017).** "Productive, anxious, lonely: 24 hours without push notifications." MobileHCI '17. https://doi.org/10.1145/3098279.3098526. 30 volunteers turned off all notification alerts for a day. They felt less distracted and more productive, but also anxious about not responding in time and less connected. Two years later, half still managed notifications the way they had planned. Small self-selected sample, one day.
- **Mehrotra, Pejovic, Vermeulen, Hendley, Musolesi (2016).** "My Phone and Me: Understanding People's Receptivity to Mobile Notifications." CHI '16. https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858566. 10,372 notifications from 20 users. How disruptive a notification felt depended on the alert type, who sent it and what the user was doing. Useful content did not make a notification less disruptive. Small sample.
- **Weber, Voit, Auda, Schneegass, Henze (2018).** "Snooze! Investigating the User-Defined Deferral of Mobile Notifications." MobileHCI '18. https://doi.org/10.1145/3229434.3229436. A year of use by 295 people of an Android app that snoozes notifications. People mostly deferred notifications about people and events, usually to later the same day or the next morning; more than two days was rare. Descriptive, no well-being outcome.
- **Ward, Duke, Gneezy, Bos (2017).** "Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity." *Journal of the Association for Consumer Research* 2(2). https://doi.org/10.1086/691462. In two experiments, having one's own phone nearby lowered available cognitive capacity, most for people who depend on it. **It did not replicate** in a preregistered direct replication (Ruiz Pardo & Minda 2022, *Acta Psychologica* 230, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103717). Hartmann, Martarelli, Reber, Rothen (2020, *Consciousness and Cognition* 86, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2020.103033) found no overall effect on short-term or prospective memory. A meta-analysis of 22 studies (Böttger, Poschik, Zierer 2023, *Behavioral Sciences* 13(9), https://doi.org/10.3390/bs13090751) found a small pooled effect (g = −0.14) that mixes phone use with presence, with high heterogeneity. Unsettled; grod does not rely on it.

## Design principles grod follows

1. **Slow the scroll, keep the app.** Messages, posts, search and single videos stay free; only scrolling costs time (Lyngs 2020: removing the whole app brings fear of missing out; Chiossi 2023: the harm comes from rapid switching).
2. **Friction that grows.** Each pause is longer than the last, because people learn to ignore a fixed pop-up (Meinhardt 2026).
3. **Always a way out.** The pause screen offers "Leave" from the first second; backing out is the behavior that changes habits (Grüning 2023).
4. **Measure behavior, not content.** grod counts scrolling, not what is on screen, so it works the same in every app and survives app redesigns. Blocking only the Reels or Shorts tab missed that the home feed now plays short videos too.
5. **Stable, explained interventions.** Rules do not rotate. The pause screen says which app and how long you scrolled (Kovacs 2018).
6. **Notifications in batches, not off.** The phone holds app notifications and delivers them together. Calls and alarms still ring, so nothing urgent is lost (Fitz 2019; Pielot & Rello 2017: alerts fully off made people anxious).
7. **Ask why, then hold the user to it.** Before a distracting app opens, one tap says what it is for. Quick purposes open at once with a time window learned from past sessions; a purpose that keeps running over loses its quick open; Browse waits, longer each time that day, and asks what comes next in the real world (MindPhone 2022; rabbit hole 2023; Wellspent 2025; LocknType 2019).
8. **The user defines distraction.** Rules and pause settings are data the user (or their agent) changes in one place (Lyngs 2020).

## Not built, with the evidence for each

- Making a rule harder to turn off than to turn on, because users drift toward weaker settings (Kovacs 2021).
- Time-of-day schedules, because context changes how well interventions work (Meinhardt 2025).
- A usage dashboard, because dashboards do not raise the sense of agency (Zhang 2022; Okeke 2018). The weekly review is a conversation with numbers, not a dashboard.
- A grayscale mode. It cut social media and browsing time but not video time (Holte 2020).
- Blocking all mobile internet. It improved attention, but only a quarter of participants kept the block on for two weeks (Castelo 2025).
